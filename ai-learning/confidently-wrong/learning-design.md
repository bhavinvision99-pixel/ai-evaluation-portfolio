# Learning Design

## Design objective

> Help a non-technical AI user recognise when an AI-generated answer deserves further scrutiny and give them a simple framework for deciding what to check.

The goal is behaviour change rather than vocabulary acquisition.

## The STOP framework

STOP was designed as both a mnemonic and a behaviour.

### S — SOURCE
**Where did this information come from?**

Ask whether there is a source, whether it can be inspected, whether it is authoritative and current, and whether it actually supports the claim.

**Key idea:** A confident answer is not a source.

### T — TRUTH
**Can the important claim be independently verified?**

Ask whether the output is a fact, interpretation or suggestion; whether the important part can be checked elsewhere; and whether the evidence justifies the conclusion.

**Key idea:** Plausibility is not proof.

### O — OVERCONFIDENCE
**Is the AI more certain than the evidence allows?**

Look for hidden assumptions, alternative explanations, correlation presented as causation, or interpretation presented as fact.

**Key idea:** Confidence is a writing style. It isn't evidence.

### P — PAUSE
**What happens if this answer is wrong?**

This introduces proportional verification. A low-stakes brainstorming request needs different scrutiny from legal, financial, medical or confidential decisions.

**Key idea:** The higher the consequence, the stronger the verification.

## Why STOP is revealed progressively

The learner initially sees `[ ? ] [ ? ] [ ? ] [ ? ]` and earns each component through the experience.

The intended progression is:

**experience the problem → make a judgement → receive feedback → discover the principle**

rather than:

**read principle → memorise principle → answer recall question**

## Plausible distractors

Incorrect answers are intentionally tempting. This better reflects the real problem users encounter with convincing AI output and forces the learner to distinguish between plausible explanations rather than spotting an obviously wrong option.

## Immediate explanatory feedback

Each challenge explains why the strongest answer wins, why distractors are tempting, and one memorable practical takeaway.

Examples:

> A confident answer is not a source.

> Plausibility is not proof.

> Confidence is a writing style. It isn't evidence.

## Active retrieval

The learner must select an answer before receiving the explanation, creating a comparison between **what I thought** and **what the evidence suggests**.

## Progressive difficulty

1. What is the system doing?
2. Where did this information come from?
3. Can the claim be verified?
4. Is the conclusion more confident than the evidence?
5. What happens if someone acts on it?

## Transfer challenge

The final interaction presents a new business recommendation containing several warning signs. The learner applies all four elements: **Source + Truth + Overconfidence + Pause**.

The goal is transfer, not simply remembering the acronym.

## Behavioural objective

**Trigger:** An AI answer is important, unusually specific or highly confident.  
**Behaviour:** The user pauses rather than immediately acting.  
**Mental checklist:** SOURCE → TRUTH → OVERCONFIDENCE → PAUSE  
**Outcome:** Verification effort becomes proportional to risk.

The desired behaviour is not constant distrust. It is **appropriate scepticism**.

## Design constraint

The experience is deliberately short — approximately five minutes — and prioritises one useful behaviour, one memorable framework, carefully designed examples and immediate application over comprehensive AI literacy coverage.

## Future learning opportunities

The architecture could extend to retrieval and grounding, RAG, prompt design, context limitations, bias, AI agents, automation, deepfakes, model selection, human oversight and AI evaluation.
