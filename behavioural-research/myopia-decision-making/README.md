# Behavioural science in myopia management

## Research question

How can behavioural science help explain and improve a difficult preventative-health decision in which a parent authorises treatment, a child must sustain the behaviour, the benefit is delayed and partly invisible, and the clinician is also part of the decision system?

## Context

This project grew from building a paediatric myopia management service in an independent UK optometry practice.

The behavioural problem was unusually demanding:

- the decision-maker was not the person receiving the treatment
- there was no symptom to relieve
- success was largely invisible because effective prevention means a bad outcome does not occur
- treatment required sustained behaviour over several years
- the clinician's own discomfort about appearing commercial could alter how the recommendation was delivered

## Approach

I used multiple behavioural frameworks as diagnostic lenses rather than as a checklist.

Key elements included:

- empathy mapping to distinguish stated objections from felt concerns
- COM-B applied separately to parent, child and clinician
- a four-part system model covering child, parent, clinician and organisation
- Horne and Weinman's Necessity-Concerns framework adapted upstream from medication adherence to pre-adoption and proxy decision-making
- the Williams model to segment resistance into scared, overwhelmed and deferring
- Value Proposition Canvas, Fogg B=MAP, MINDSPACE, EAST, See-Think-Do-Care and other behavioural tools
- a continuous listening loop using parent and child feedback
- end-to-end journey mapping from first concern through consultation and long-term adherence

## Central hypothesis

Necessity and concern were treated as independent variables.

The intervention sequence was therefore designed to reduce concern before increasing necessity, and to let parents generate their own reasons for action rather than relying on clinician pressure.

A second design move was to lead with immediate, verifiable benefits for the child rather than relying only on distant, probabilistic prevention.

## Intervention architecture

The service was designed as four connected layers:

1. **Pre-consultation decision shaping** through stage-matched content and third-party authority.
2. **Structured consultation** with a repeatable sequence intended to resolve concern before asking for a decision.
3. **Decision-point design** using prompt timing, messenger, salience, defaults, commitment and friction reduction.
4. **Adherence loop** with reinforcement, scheduled contact and visible progress feedback.

## Observed outcomes

The service reached:

- approximately **80% consultation-to-enrolment conversion**
- **under 6% attrition across four years**
- no paid advertising during the growth period

The case study also reports that 79% of children seen in their first year on the programme showed no measurable prescription change. This was observational practice data, not a controlled comparison.

## Research limitations

The original work was applied practice research rather than a controlled experiment.

Important limitations identified in the case study include:

- qualitative listening data cannot establish causal mechanisms
- necessity and concern were not measured quantitatively before and after consultation
- discontinuation reasons were not coded systematically
- parents who declined were not studied in the same depth as those who enrolled
- the intervention combined several behavioural mechanisms, making individual causal attribution difficult

## What I would test next

A stronger follow-up study would:

- quantify necessity and concern before and after consultation
- use an adapted validated instrument
- compare structured and control consultation approaches
- code reasons for discontinuation
- study families who decline as well as those who enrol
- define behaviour-change techniques formally before deployment
